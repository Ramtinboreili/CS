1. Function and limit
@@ c2-1|body#1
A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ maps each input to one output. The <b>limit</b> $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ means that as $x$ approaches $a$, $f(x)$ approaches $L$ (the ε–δ definition: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that $0<|x-a|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$). Continuity means $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.

Why is $-\nabla f$ the direction of steepest descent?
@@ c2-1|body#11
The <b>directional derivative</b> along the unit vector $\mathbf u$: $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$ (first-order approximation: $f(\mathbf x+t\mathbf u)\approx f(\mathbf x)+t\,\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$). This is maximal for $\theta=0$ (along the gradient, $+\|\nabla f\|$) and minimal for $\theta=\pi$ (against the gradient, $-\|\nabla f\|$). So $\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest increase and $-\nabla f$ the direction of steepest decrease (steepest descent). The gradient is also perpendicular to the <b>level curves</b> ($f=\text{constant}$) because along a tangent to a level curve $D_{\mathbf u}f=0$.

2. The Jacobian matrix
@@ c2-2|body#3
For $\mathbf f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ the <b>Jacobian</b> is the $m\times n$ matrix of all partial derivatives: $J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$. The best linear approximation of the function near a point: $\mathbf f(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+J\boldsymbol\delta$. <b>The matrix chain rule:</b> for the composition $\mathbf h=\mathbf f\circ\mathbf g$: $J_{\mathbf h}=J_{\mathbf f}\,J_{\mathbf g}$ (matrix multiplication). For a scalar loss $L$ and a linear layer $\mathbf y=W\mathbf x$: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf x}=W^T\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf y}$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf y}\mathbf x^T$.

3. Backpropagation
@@ c2-2|body#5
A neural network is a deep composition of simple functions: $L=\ell(f_k(\cdots f_2(f_1(\mathbf x))))$. Computing $\nabla_\theta L$ efficiently needs two passes: <b>forward</b> (storing the intermediate values) and <b>backward</b> (applying the chain rule from output to input). Since the cost of the backward pass is of the same order as the forward pass, the gradient of all parameters is obtained at about the cost of running the network twice (reverse-mode autodiff). 1. Foundations
@@ c3-1|body#1
The <b>sample space</b> $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes; an <b>event</b> is $A\subseteq\Omega$. The probability function $P$ satisfies Kolmogorov's axioms: (1) $P(A)\ge0$; (2) $P(\Omega)=1$; (3) for mutually exclusive events $P(\cup A_i)=\sum P(A_i)$. Consequences: $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$ and $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$. In the equally likely case: $P(A)=|A|/|\Omega|$ (for example the sum of two dice equal to 7 ⇒ $6/36=1/6$).
@@ c3-1|body#2
2. Conditional probability and independence
@@ c3-1|body#3
$P(A\mid B)$ is the probability of $A$ knowing that $B$ occurred:
@@ c3-1|body#4
Intuition: we restrict the sample space to $B$ and renormalize. <b>The product rule:</b> $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$ and for chains: $P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_n)=\prod P(A_i\mid A_1,\dots,A_{i-1})$ (the basis of autoregressive language models). <b>Independence:</b> $A,B$ are independent if $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$; equivalently $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ (knowing $B$ says nothing about $A$). "Mutually exclusive" differs from "independent": two mutually exclusive events with positive probability are never independent. <b>Conditional independence</b> ($A\perp B\mid C$) is the key assumption of naive Bayes.
