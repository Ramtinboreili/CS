1. The random variable
@@ c3-2|body#1
A <b>random variable</b> is a function that maps each outcome of a random experiment to a number: $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. <b>Discrete:</b> countably many values; with a <b>probability mass function</b> $p(x)=P(X=x)$ where $\sum p(x)=1$. <b>Continuous:</b> with a <b>probability density function</b> $f(x)\ge0$ and $\int f=1$; the probability of an interval is the area under the density: $P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bf(x)dx$ and for any single point $P(X=x)=0$ (a density can exceed 1!). The <b>cumulative distribution function (CDF)</b>: $F(x)=P(X\le x)$, non-decreasing with limits 0 and 1.

2. Expectation and variance
@@ c3-2|body#3

$$E[X]=\sum_xx\,p(x)\quad\Big(\text{or}\ \int xf(x)dx\Big),\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=E\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]=E[X^2]-\mu^2,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$$

@@ c3-2|body#4
<b>Deriving the shortcut formula:</b> $E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]=E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2$. <b>Linearity of expectation:</b> $E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c$ (even without independence!). Variance: $\mathrm{Var}(aX+c)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)$ and for independent variables $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)$. <b>Example:</b> a fair die: $E[X]=3.5$; $E[X^2]=\frac{91}{6}$; $\mathrm{Var}=\frac{91}{6}-3.5^2\approx2.917$.
@@ c3-2|body#5
3. Covariance and correlation
@@ c3-2|body#6
$\mathrm{Cov}>0$: when $X$ gets bigger, $Y$ usually does too. Independent ⇒ $\mathrm{Cov}=0$ but not the other way round (example: $Y=X^2$ with $X$ symmetric about zero). For a random vector, the <b>covariance matrix</b> $\Sigma_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)$ is symmetric and positive semi-definite — the very matrix whose eigenvalues PCA takes.

4. Key distributions
@@ c3-2|body#8
Bernoulli
@@ c3-2|body#9
$X\in\{0,1\}$ with $P(X=1)=p$. $E=p$, $\mathrm{Var}=p(1-p)$. The model of one binary label; the sigmoid output in binary classification is the parameter $p$.
@@ c3-2|body#10
Binomial
@@ c3-2|body#11
The number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials: $P(K=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$; $E=np$ and $\mathrm{Var}=np(1-p)$ (because it is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoullis and the variances add).
@@ c3-2|body#12
Gaussian (normal)
@@ c3-2|body#13
Bell-shaped and symmetric about $\mu$; determined by two parameters $\mu,\sigma$. The 68–95–99.7 rule: $P(|X-\mu|<k\sigma)\approx0.683,\ 0.954,\ 0.997$ for $k=1,2,3$. <b>Standardization:</b> $Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Why it is everywhere: the <b>central limit theorem</b> (a sum/mean of independent variables tends to normal) and it has the maximum entropy for a given mean and variance. In ML: weight initialization, noise in regression (which leads to MSE), VAE, diffusion.

The idea
@@ c3-3|body#1
You have a probabilistic model with parameter $\theta$ and observed data $\mathcal{D}=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$. Which $\theta$ makes the data "more probable"? The <b>likelihood</b> is a function of $\theta$ for the fixed data: for independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
@@ c3-3|body#2
A subtle difference: $p(x\mid\theta)$ for a fixed $\theta$ is a distribution over $x$ (sum/integral 1); $L(\theta)$ for a fixed $x$ is a function of $\theta$ and not necessarily a distribution. Because the product becomes extremely small (underflow) and differentiating a product is hard, we use the <b>logarithm</b> (an increasing function that does not move the maximum): $\ell(\theta)=\sum_i\ln p(x_i\mid\theta)$.

(The second derivative is negative ⇒ a maximum.) That is, the MLE is the observed proportion. <b>Warning:</b> with 3 tosses and 3 heads, $\hat p=1$, which is unreasonable; the MLE has no prior and exaggerates for small samples. (MAP with a prior or Laplace smoothing fixes this.)

Example 2: Gaussian
@@ c3-3|body#8
$x_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\ell(\mu,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2$. Derivative with respect to $\mu$: $\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)=0\Rightarrow\hat\mu=\bar x$. Derivative with respect to $\sigma^2$: $-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2=0\Rightarrow\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$. This variance estimate is <b>biased</b> ($E[\hat\sigma^2]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$); the unbiased version divides by $n-1$.
@@ c3-3|body#9
Relation to ML loss functions
@@ c3-3|body#10
<b>Regression with Gaussian noise:</b> $y=f_\theta(x)+\varepsilon,\ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ ⇒ $-\ell(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-f_\theta(x_i))^2+\text{const}$ ⇒ <b>maximizing the likelihood = minimizing the MSE</b>.
@@ c3-3|body#11
<b>Binary classification:</b> $P(y=1|x)=\sigma(f_\theta(x))$ ⇒ $-\ell=-\sum\big[y\ln\hat p+(1-y)\ln(1-\hat p)\big]$ = <b>binary cross-entropy</b>. For multiclass with softmax: categorical cross-entropy.
@@ c3-3|body#12
The <b>negative log-likelihood (NLL)</b> is the standard loss; the training error of language models (perplexity = $e^{\text{NLL}}$) is the same.
@@ c3-3|body#13
Properties of the MLE
@@ c3-3|body#14
Under regularity assumptions: <b>consistent</b> (it tends to the true $\theta$ as $n\to\infty$), <b>asymptotically normal</b> and <b>asymptotically efficient</b>; but for small $n$ it may be biased or overfit. Adding a prior (MAP): $\hat\theta_{MAP}=\arg\max[\ell(\theta)+\ln p(\theta)]$ — a Gaussian prior ⇒ an L2 penalty, a Laplace prior ⇒ L1.

1. Descriptive statistics
@@ c4-1|body#1
The <b>mean</b> $\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_i$ (sensitive to outliers), the <b>median</b> (the middle value; robust), the <b>mode</b> (the most frequent).
@@ c4-1|body#2
The <b>sample variance</b> $s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ and the standard deviation $s$. Dividing by $n-1$ (<b>Bessel's correction</b>) makes the estimate unbiased because $\bar x$ itself is estimated from this data and has used up one degree of freedom.
@@ c4-1|body#3
<b>Quartiles and the IQR:</b> $Q_3-Q_1$; the outlier rule: outside $[Q_1-1.5\,IQR,\ Q_3+1.5\,IQR]$.
@@ c4-1|body#4
<b>Skewness and kurtosis:</b> the shape of the distribution.
@@ c4-1|body#5
<b>Example:</b> the data 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9: mean $5$, median $4.5$, mode 4; $s^2=\frac{9+1+1+1+0+0+4+16}{7}=4.57$, $s\approx2.14$. Adding the outlier 90 makes the mean jump to ≈13.3 but the median only reaches 5.
@@ c4-1|body#6
2. Population and sample
@@ c4-1|body#7
A <b>population</b> is the set of all units with fixed parameters ($\mu,\sigma$); a <b>sample</b> is the subset we measure and the <b>statistics</b> ($\bar x,s$) estimate the parameters. Sampling must be <b>random and representative</b>; <b>sampling bias</b> (selection bias, survivorship bias) is not cured by increasing the sample size. In ML: the training set is a sample of the true distribution; if the test distribution differs (distribution shift) performance drops.
@@ c4-1|body#8
3. The central limit theorem and the standard error
@@ c4-1|body#9
If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then for large $n$ $\bar X\approx\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$ <b>regardless of the original distribution</b>. The <b>standard error</b> is the standard deviation of the sample mean: $SE=\sigma/\sqrt n$. To double the precision, four times the data is needed (the $1/\sqrt n$ law).

The central limit theorem: the distribution of the sample mean for different populations

4. The confidence interval (CI)
@@ c4-1|body#12
For the mean with known $\sigma$: $\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$ ($z=1.96$ for 95%). With unknown $\sigma$ use $s$ and the $t$ distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom: $\bar x\pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}$ (for small $n$ the $t$ has heavier tails). <b>Example:</b> $n=100,\ \bar x=52,\ s=10$: $SE=1$; the 95% CI ≈ $52\pm1.96=[50.04,\,53.96]$.
@@ c4-1|body#13
<b>The correct interpretation:</b> "a method that, 95% of the time, builds an interval that covers μ". μ is fixed and the interval is random; once a specific interval is built you cannot say "with 95% probability μ is in it".

5. Use in ML
@@ c4-1|body#16
Reporting model accuracy without uncertainty is misleading: with 90% accuracy on 100 test samples, $SE=\sqrt{0.9\cdot0.1/100}=0.03$ and the 95% CI ≈ $[84\%,96\%]$. Comparing two models that differ by 1% may be within the noise. <b>Bootstrap</b> methods (resampling the data with replacement) give a CI without assuming a distribution.
