Linear regression with matrices
@@ x6|body#1
$m$ samples with $n$ features: the design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n+1)}$ (a column of ones for the intercept), $\mathbf y\in\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbf w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. Predicting all samples is one matrix–vector product: $\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w$. The squared cost: $J(\mathbf w)=\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2$. Setting the gradient $\nabla J=-2X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)$ to zero:
@@ x6|body#2
<b>Geometric interpretation:</b> $X\mathbf w$ always lies in the <b>column space</b> of $X$; the best $\hat{\mathbf y}$ is the point of that space closest to $\mathbf y$, i.e. the <b>orthogonal projection</b> of $\mathbf y$ onto it; hence the error $\mathbf y-X\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to all columns of $X$ ($X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)=0$) — the normal equation. (The link to the projection and orthogonality lesson.)

The dense layer and batches
@@ x6|body#5
A fully connected layer with $n_{in}$ inputs and $n_{out}$ outputs: $\mathbf y=W\mathbf x+\mathbf b$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{out}\times n_{in}}$. For a batch of $B$ samples: $Y=XW^T+\mathbf b$ ($B\times n_{in}$ times $n_{in}\times n_{out}$). The whole training and inference of an MLP is a chain of matrix multiplications and nonlinear functions. The cost of each layer is $B\cdot n_{in}\cdot n_{out}$ multiplications; GPUs are built exactly for this operation (GEMM).

The training loop
@@ x7|body#1
<b>Forward:</b> from the input to the output $\hat y$ (matrix-multiplication functions + nonlinearities like tanh/ReLU).
@@ x7|body#2
<b>Loss:</b> $J=\frac1n\sum\ell(\hat y,y)$ (for example cross-entropy).
@@ x7|body#3
<b>Backward:</b> the chain rule, $\nabla_\theta J$ for all the weights.
@@ x7|body#4
<b>Update:</b> $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla J$ (or Adam...).

@@ x8|body#2
<b>Precision</b> is the posterior probability $P(y{=}1\mid\hat y{=}1)$ — exactly Bayes' theorem's quantity; so it depends on the <b>prevalence (prior)</b>: with recall and FPR fixed, lowering the prevalence makes precision collapse (the medical-test example). Threshold, ROC and AUC
@@ x8|body#4
The model gives a continuous score; the threshold $\tau$ turns it into a label. As $\tau$ changes, the (FPR, TPR) pairs trace the <b>ROC</b> curve. The <b>AUC</b> equals the probability that a random positive sample gets a higher score than a random negative one (a probabilistic interpretation; $0.5$ = random guessing, $1$ = perfect separation). AUC does not depend on the threshold and (roughly) not on the prevalence; but at very low prevalence the precision–recall curve is more informative. In the widget we assume the two classes' scores are two Gaussians with means $0$ and $d'$.

Metric uncertainty
@@ x8|body#7
Every metric on test data is an <b>estimate</b>: an accuracy $\hat p$ on $n$ samples has standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. Comparing two models must be done with a statistical test or CI (bootstrap, McNemar's test), not only by numeric difference. And <b>data leakage</b> invalidates the validation.

What is an embedding?
@@ x9|body#1
A computer works with numbers; words, sentences, images and users must be mapped to <b>dense vectors</b> (say 300 to 4096 dimensions) so that <b>semantic similarity = geometric closeness</b>. This mapping (Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT, text-embedding models) is learned from data: words that occur in similar contexts get close vectors ("you shall know a word by the company it keeps").
@@ x9|body#2
Similarity measures
@@ x9|body#3
<b>The dot product:</b> $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$ (also sensitive to length).
@@ x9|body#4
<b>Cosine similarity:</b> $\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|}$ is sensitive only to direction; the standard for semantic search. If the vectors are normalized ($\|\mathbf a\|=1$) it equals the dot product and a search becomes one matrix product: $\text{scores}=E\,\mathbf q$.
@@ x9|body#5
<b>Euclidean distance:</b> $\|\mathbf a-\mathbf b\|$; for unit vectors $\|\mathbf a-\mathbf b\|^2=2-2\cos\theta$ holds.

From embedding to attention
@@ x10|body#1
The input of a transformer is a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$: $n$ tokens, each a $d$-dimensional embedding vector. The goal of attention: each token "retrieves" relevant information from the other tokens. Three learned linear transformations:
@@ x10|body#2
<b>Intuition:</b> "query" = "what am I looking for?", "key" = "what do I have?", "value" = "what I give if you select me". The similarity of each token's query with the keys of all tokens (the dot product, the same similarity concept!) builds the attention weights:
@@ x10|body#3
$S=QK^T$ is the $n\times n$ matrix of dot products: $S_{ij}=\mathbf q_i\cdot\mathbf k_j$.
@@ x10|body#4
Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$: if the components are independent with variance 1, $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k)=d_k$; dividing keeps the variance at 1 and prevents softmax saturation (vanishing gradient).
@@ x10|body#5
Row-wise softmax: each row a probability distribution (sum 1) over the tokens.
@@ x10|body#6
$O=AV$: a linear combination of the rows of $V$ with the attention weights.
@@ x10|body#7
Attention with real matrices: scaling, causal mask and weights
@@ x10|body#8
The causal mask and multi-head
@@ x10|body#9
In autoregressive language models (GPT) a token must not see the future: before the softmax, $-\infty$ is added to the entries above the diagonal (weight 0). <b>Multi-head:</b> $h$ independent sets $(W_Q,W_K,W_V)$ run in parallel and the outputs are concatenated and combined with $W_O$; each head learns a different pattern (syntax, reference, ...).
@@ x10|body#10
Cost
@@ x10|body#11
The products $XW_Q$ and so on cost $O(nd^2)$, and $QK^T$ and $AV$ cost $O(n^2d)$ with $O(n^2)$ memory. So doubling the text length ≈ quadruples the attention cost (the reason for context-window limits) and motivates methods like FlashAttention (reducing memory I/O), sparse/linear attention and the KV cache at inference. The rest of the transformer (the feed-forward network) is also a few big matrix products; so the whole model is mainly a chain of GEMMs on a GPU.
@@ x10|body#12
<b class="t">Where the three subjects meet</b>Matrix multiplication (linear algebra) + softmax/probability distribution (probability) + differentiability for training (calculus) + tensor memory layout and the cache (data structures): the transformer has all of these in one place.

Show that if $\mathbf q,\mathbf k$ have independent components with mean 0 and variance 1 then $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k)=d_k$, and what this implies for the scaling.
@@ x10|ex:3:a#0
$\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k=\sum_{i=1}^{d_k}q_ik_i$. Each term has $E[q_ik_i]=0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(q_ik_i)=E[q_i^2]E[k_i^2]=1$ (independence); the terms are independent so $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k)=d_k$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{d_k}$. Without division the scores grow with $d_k$ and the softmax becomes nearly one-hot (negligible gradient); dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the score variance at 1.
