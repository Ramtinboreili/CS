1. The logic of hypothesis testing
@@ c4-2|body#1
We want to judge a claim using data. The <b>null hypothesis $H_0$</b> is the "no effect/no difference" situation and the <b>alternative $H_1$</b> is our claim. Method: assume $H_0$ is true, build a <b>test statistic</b> from the data and ask: "if $H_0$ were true, how rare would data <i>at least this extreme</i> be?" This probability is the <b>p-value</b>. If $p<\alpha$ (usually 0.05) we reject $H_0$.
@@ c4-2|body#2
<b>The z-test for a mean:</b> $z=\dfrac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$, which under $H_0$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; the two-sided p-value $=2\big(1-\Phi(|z|)\big)$. <b>Example:</b> $\mu_0=100,\ \sigma=15,\ n=36,\ \bar x=105$: $z=\frac{5}{2.5}=2$ ⇒ $p\approx0.0455<0.05$ ⇒ reject $H_0$.

3. Correlation and causation
@@ c4-2|body#16
The Pearson correlation coefficient $r=\dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{s_Xs_Y}\in[-1,1]$ measures only <b>linear</b> association and is sensitive to outliers. $r^2$ is the share of variance explained in simple linear regression. <b>Correlation is not causation:</b> possible reasons: reverse causation, a <b>confounder</b> (for example air temperature raises both ice-cream sales and drownings), chance and Simpson's paradox. Causation is established by randomized experiments (RCTs) or causal-inference methods.

4. The bias–variance trade-off
@@ c4-2|body#19
For a target $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$ ($E[\varepsilon]=0,\ \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$) and a learned model $\hat f$, the expected squared error at the point $x$:
@@ c4-2|body#20

$$E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}+\underbrace{E\big[(\hat f(x)-E[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}$$

@@ c4-2|body#21
<b>Bias:</b> the error from overly simple assumptions (underfitting). <b>Variance:</b> the sensitivity of $\hat f$ to the training sample (overfitting). A simple model ⇒ high bias/low variance; a complex model ⇒ the opposite; the test error is U-shaped. Remedies: more data (variance↓), regularization, early stopping, ensembles/bagging (variance↓), more capacity (bias↓). <i>A modern note:</i> in very large networks the "double descent" phenomenon is observed, but the decomposition above still holds.

@@ c5-1|body#14

@@ c5-1|body#15

@@ c5-1|body#16

1. Stochastic gradient and mini-batches
@@ c5-2|body#1
In batch GD each step reads all $n$ samples: $\nabla J=\frac1n\sum\nabla\ell_i$. For $n$ in the millions this is expensive. <b>SGD</b> approximates the gradient with one random sample and <b>mini-batch</b> with a batch of $B$ (say 32–512). The mini-batch gradient is a <b>noisy unbiased estimate</b> of the true gradient: $E[\hat g]=\nabla J$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat g)\propto1/B$. Consequences:
@@ c5-2|body#2
Each step is cheaper ⇒ more updates per epoch;
@@ c5-2|body#3
the gradient noise can help escape sharp local minima/saddle points and often ends in "flatter" minima (better generalization);
@@ c5-2|body#4
parallelization on GPUs works well with large batches; but a very large batch may worsen generalization and require a larger η (the linear scaling rule).
@@ c5-2|body#5
<b>Epoch:</b> one full pass over the data. SGD noise means that with a constant η it does not converge exactly to the minimum and oscillates around it; for exact convergence η must decrease.

@@ c5-2|body#12

@@ c5-3|body#29

@@ c5-3|body#30
(equivalently $H\succeq0$ for a twice-differentiable function). <b>The key consequence:</b> every local minimum of a convex function is a <b>global</b> minimum (and unique if <i>strictly</i> convex); GD with a suitable η reaches it. @@ c5-3|ex:4:q#0
Prove that $f(x)=x^4-3x^2+x$ is not convex and say what this implies for GD.
@@ c5-3|ex:4:a#0
$f''(x)=12x^2-6$, which is negative for $|x|<1/\sqrt2$ (for example $-6$ at $x=0$) ⇒ the Hessian is not positive definite and the function is non-convex. The function has two local minima (one near $x\approx-1.3$ which is global and another near $x\approx1.1$). Depending on the start (and η) GD reaches one of them; from a positive start it may stay in the (worse) local minimum. Hence random restarts, momentum or noise are needed.
