1. Scalar, vector, matrix, tensor
@@ c1-1|body#1
A <b>scalar</b> is a single number ($x\in\mathbb{R}$). A <b>vector</b> is an ordered list of $n$ numbers: $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$; it can be both a "point" and an "arrow from the origin" and in ML is usually the "feature vector" of a sample. A <b>matrix</b> $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is a rectangular table with $m$ rows and $n$ columns (for example a data set of $m$ samples with $n$ features, or the weights of a layer). A <b>tensor</b> generalizes to $k$ indices: a colour image $H\times W\times3$, a batch of images $B\times H\times W\times C$, or a transformer layer's output of shape $B\times T\times d$. The number of indices is called the <b>rank (order)</b>; a scalar has order 0, a vector 1, a matrix 2.

3. Matrix multiplication
@@ c1-1|body#12
If $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ then $C=AB\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$ with
@@ c1-1|body#13
that is, entry $(i,j)$ is the dot product of <b>row $i$ of $A$</b> with <b>column $j$ of $B$</b>. Condition: the columns of $A$ = the rows of $B$. Properties: associative ($(AB)C=A(BC)$), distributive, but <b>not commutative</b> ($AB\ne BA$ in general). Other interpretations: the columns of $AB$ are linear combinations of the columns of $A$; and $A\mathbf x$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$ with coefficients $\mathbf x$. The computational cost of multiplying $m\times n$ by $n\times p$ is $mnp$ multiplications.

4. Vector space, linear independence and basis
@@ c1-1|body#17
A <b>linear combination</b> $\sum c_i\mathbf v_i$. The <b>span</b> is the set of all linear combinations. Vectors are <b>linearly independent</b> if none is a linear combination of the others (that is, $\sum c_i\mathbf v_i=0\Rightarrow$ all $c_i=0$). A <b>basis</b> is a linearly independent set that spans the whole space; its size is the <b>dimension</b> of the space. For example $\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$ is the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ but $\{(1,2),(2,4)\}$ are not independent and span only a line. ML importance: dependent columns in the feature matrix (collinearity) make regression unstable; and embeddings are points in a vector space with a learned basis.

2. The determinant
@@ c1-2|body#16
For a $2\times2$ matrix: $\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc$. <b>Geometric interpretation:</b> $|\det A|$ is the factor by which area (volume in higher dimensions) changes under the transformation; its sign shows orientation ($\det<0$ means a reflection). The unit square with sides $(a,c)$ and $(b,d)$ becomes a parallelogram of area $|ad-bc|$. $\det A=0$ means the transformation squashes space into a lower dimension (singular). Properties: $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$, $\det A^T=\det A$.
@@ c1-2|body#17
3. The inverse matrix
@@ c1-2|body#18
$A^{-1}$ undoes the transformation: $A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I$. An inverse exists ⇔ $\det A\ne0$ ⇔ the columns are linearly independent. For $2\times2$:
@@ c1-2|body#19
<b>Example:</b> $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$: $\det=1$ ⇒ $A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Check: $AA^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2-1&-2+2\\1-1&-1+2\end{pmatrix}=I$ ✓. A practical note: in numerical computation we never form $A^{-1}$ explicitly; we solve the system by Gaussian elimination/LU decomposition (more stable and cheaper).

4. Rank and systems of linear equations
@@ c1-2|body#21
The <b>rank</b> equals the dimension of the column space = the number of independent columns. $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ has rank $r\le\min(m,n)$. The system $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$: it has a solution if $\mathbf b$ is in the column space; if $A$ is square with full rank, the unique solution is $\mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b$; if the rank is lower, infinitely many solutions or none. In linear regression, the normal equation $X^TX\mathbf w=X^T\mathbf y$ has a unique solution only if $X$ has independent columns; otherwise $X^TX$ is non-invertible (the reason for regularization/ridge, which makes $X^TX+\lambda I$ invertible).
@@ c1-2|body#22
The <b>condition number</b> $\kappa(A)=\sigma_{max}/\sigma_{min}$ shows how "close to singular" a matrix is; a large $\kappa$ means a small input error becomes a large output error.

1. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors
@@ c1-3|body#1
Most vectors change direction under the transformation $A$. Some special vectors are only <b>stretched</b> (their direction stays on the same line): $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$, with $\mathbf v\ne0$. $\mathbf v$ is an eigenvector and $\lambda$ an eigenvalue. Interpretation: $\lambda$ is the stretch factor in that direction (negative = direction reversal). <b>Computation:</b> $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=0$ has a non-zero solution ⇔ $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ (the characteristic equation).
@@ c1-3|body#2
<b>Example:</b> $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. $\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)$ ⇒ $\lambda_1=3,\ \lambda_2=1$. For $\lambda=3$: $(A-3I)\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v=0\Rightarrow\mathbf v_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2$. For $\lambda=1$: $\mathbf v_2=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. Note: $\mathrm{tr}A=\lambda_1+\lambda_2=4$ and $\det A=\lambda_1\lambda_2=3$.
@@ c1-3|body#3
Select the symmetric matrix [[2,1],[1,2]] from the presets and see the eigenvector lines
@@ c1-3|body#4
<b>The spectral theorem:</b> a real <b>symmetric</b> matrix always has real eigenvalues and <b>orthogonal</b> eigenvectors: $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal ($Q^TQ=I$) and $\Lambda$ diagonal. Rotations (non-symmetric matrices) can give complex eigenvalues (like a 90° rotation).

2. The singular value decomposition (SVD)
@@ c1-3|body#6
Every matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ (square or not) has a decomposition:
@@ c1-3|body#7

$$A=U\Sigma V^T,\quad U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\ V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\ \text{orthogonal},\quad \Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0)$$

@@ c1-3|body#8
<b>Geometric intuition:</b> every linear transformation = "rotation ($V^T$) ← stretch along the axes ($\Sigma$) ← rotation ($U$)". That is, the unit circle becomes an <b>ellipse</b> with semi-axes $\sigma_i$ (purple in the widget above). <b>Relation to eigenvalues:</b> $A^TA=V\Sigma^2V^T$ so $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}$ and the columns of $V$ are eigenvectors of $A^TA$. The $\sigma_i$ are always non-negative and exist for every matrix, unlike eigenvalues.
@@ c1-3|body#9
Low-rank approximation
@@ c1-3|body#10
$A=\sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^T$ is a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices. Keeping the first $k$ terms is the best rank-$k$ approximation in the Frobenius norm (the <b>Eckart–Young theorem</b>):
@@ c1-3|body#11
Uses: <b>image compression</b> (a $1000\times1000$ matrix with $k=50$ needs only $50(1000+1000+1)\approx10^5$ numbers instead of $10^6$), <b>denoising</b>, <b>LSA</b> in NLP, and the <b>LoRA</b> method for fine-tuning large models, which assumes the weight update is low-rank.
@@ c1-3|body#12
3. PCA (principal component analysis)
@@ c1-3|body#13
Consider the centred data $X$ ($m\times n$). The covariance matrix $C=\frac{1}{m-1}X^TX$ is symmetric. The <b>principal components</b> are the eigenvectors of $C$ and the eigenvalues give the variance in that direction; equivalently the columns of $V$ in the SVD of $X$ ($\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2/(m-1)$). Dimensionality reduction: project the data onto the first $k$ components: $Z=XV_k$. This is the best linear projection in terms of maximizing the variance retained.
